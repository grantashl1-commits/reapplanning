# REAP — Gambling Act Framing Strategy
## How to Position the Prize Structure Outside the Gambling Act 2003
**Prepared:** April 2026 | Sport Waikato / The Living Lab
**Classification:** Internal strategy document — do not publish participant-facing

---

## The Three-Element Test

The Gambling Act 2003 defines gambling as requiring **all three** of these simultaneously:

1. Payment of consideration
2. An element of **chance**
3. A prize

REAP cannot eliminate (1) — participants pay $13. It cannot eliminate (3) — draws are a product feature. The framing strategy does not try to eliminate the element of chance in the prize outcome. It takes a different approach entirely: **it establishes that the $13 payment is not made in consideration for the element of chance.** This is the consideration test, and it is the correct legal foundation for REAP.

---

## Why the Performance Award Model Doesn't Work for REAP

An earlier version of this compliance strategy proposed awarding prizes to the participant who most demonstrably performed during each measurement period — most minutes, longest streak, highest session count. This approach was abandoned for three structural reasons:

**1. REAP's eligibility is binary.** You either logged 21+ minutes or you were eliminated. The game does not create a performance gradient among survivors — it creates a binary of alive/not-alive. There is no verifiable distinction between a survivor who logged 22 minutes and one who logged 200 minutes.

**2. Every survivor has an identical streak.** At Day 13, every survivor has survived 13 consecutive days. Nobody has a "longer" streak than anyone else. The streak criterion doesn't differentiate.

**3. REAP doesn't accumulate total minutes.** The app tracks whether the daily threshold was met, not how far above the threshold participants performed. Total season minutes are not a metric the system holds.

Trying to retrofit performance criteria onto a binary-eligibility game would require changing the game mechanic to track something it doesn't track, and would create prizes that reward a sub-game that isn't REAP. The honest structure is: **you're alive, you're in the draw, a winner is chosen randomly.** That is the structure. The compliance question is whether that structure is gambling.

## The Correct Model: Spot Prize Competition Subsidiary to a Genuine Game Product

The law does not say "any random draw for a paying participant = gambling." It says gambling requires consideration paid *for* the element of chance. The critical question is: what does the $13 buy?

**The $13 buys a game.** "Survive the Reap" is a 30-day daily-movement survival game. That is the product. Game access, activity tracking, the Survival Board, community features, the daily elimination mechanic — that is what participants are purchasing. They are not purchasing a lottery ticket.

**Surviving the game generates automatic entry into promotional draws.** At seven milestone points, Sport Waikato runs spot prize draws as a promotional benefit attached to being alive in the game. Survivors did not pay to enter these draws. They paid to play the game. Being alive when a draw runs is a consequence of playing the game well, not consideration separately paid for a chance to win.

This is the same model as:
- A supermarket runs "spend $50 and enter to win" — the payment is for groceries, the draw is promotional
- A coffee chain enters Gold members in monthly draws — the payment is for coffee, the draw is a loyalty benefit  
- McDonald's Monopoly — the payment is for a meal, the prize game is attached as a promotional benefit
- A petrol station prints instant-win codes on receipts — the payment is for fuel, the prize is incidental

In every case: genuine commercial product as primary, prize draw as subsidiary promotional benefit. The law — and the DIA — treats this as structurally different from gambling, where the payment IS the stake for the chance to win.

## The Legal Architecture

**The consideration test.** Why did participants pay? They paid to play a survival game. The subscription is a commitment device — participants want the daily accountability and the community, and they accept the elimination risk because that consequence is what makes the game work. The prize draw is a promotional benefit, not the reason they subscribed. The product's entire design, marketing, and onboarding confirm this.

**The subsidiarity test.** Is the draw subsidiary to a genuine primary activity? Yes. "Survive the Reap" is a fully realised product with its own mechanic, its own community, and its own motivation system entirely independent of the draws. The game would be worth playing without the draws. This is the critical distinction from a lottery, where there is no product — only the chance.

**Prize funding independence.** Prize money comes entirely from Sport Waikato's operational funds, completely separate from subscription revenue at the accounting level. No participant's $13 contributes to or pools toward any prize. This eliminates the "pooled consideration" structure that defines a lottery.

**No additional consideration at draw points.** Participants do not pay extra at each draw. There is no "enter the draw" button attached to a payment. Being alive is sufficient and automatic eligibility. This is essential — any payment required at the draw point would compromise the model.

## The Correct Language Framework

The new model accepts the random draw as real and lawful. The language job is to frame it accurately — as a promotional benefit attached to a game product, not as the reason people pay.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|-------|------------|
| "Survive the month to win" | "Survive the month to complete the challenge" |
| "Your $13 entry includes a chance to win" | Never — this directly links fee to prize chance |
| "Win up to $666 from $13/month" | Never — same reason |
| "Lucky draw" | "Spot prize draw" |
| "Lottery" | Never — implies gambling |
| "Prize draw entry included with subscription" | "Active survivors are automatically eligible for spot prize draws" |
| Any prize amount in headlines or CTAs | Prize amounts in Season Rules and Terms only |

**What you can say correctly:**

> *"Active survivors are automatically entered in spot prize draws at milestone points throughout the season. No additional payment is required. Draws are a promotional feature of the Survive the Reap game — the game itself is the product, and the draws are a bonus for getting this far."*

**The one-sentence board summary:**

> *"People pay $13 to play a survival game. If they're alive when a draw runs, they're automatically in it — no extra charge. Same model as any promotional competition attached to a product. The prizes come from Sport Waikato's own funds, not from participant fees."*

## Subsidiarity in Practice: What the Product Must Demonstrate

The subsidiarity test is not just a legal argument — it must be visible in the actual product. The DIA will look at whether the game is real and primary or whether it is a thin wrapper around what is functionally a lottery.

REAP passes this test on current design. The features that matter:

| Feature | Status | Why it matters |
|---------|--------|----------------|
| Game mechanic operates independently of draws | Yes — daily elimination runs whether or not a draw is nearby | Shows the game is a real product |
| Primary marketing leads with game, not prize | Yes — "Miss 21 minutes. Get eliminated." is the headline | Shows draw is subsidiary |
| Prize dollar amounts absent from hero/CTA positions | Yes — current app and proposed marketing confirmed | Shows product is primary pitch |
| Participants' primary motivation is loss aversion, not prize acquisition | Yes — the $13 stake and elimination mechanic drive behaviour | Shows draw is incidental |
| Sport Waikato's charitable mandate is health, not prize promotion | Yes — confirmed | Shows overall purpose |

**One phrase that still must be removed before launch:** Any variation of *"survive the month to win"* — this directly frames the prize as the explicit reward for completing the paid activity, which creates the precise link between the fee and the prize that the subsidiary framing must avoid. Replace with "survive the month to complete the challenge" or equivalent.

## The DIA Informal Enquiry

Sport Waikato should make an informal approach to the DIA before launch. The questions should be framed around the spot prize / subsidiary model:

1. Does DIA consider a product where participants pay for a game and active survivors are automatically entered in periodic promotional draws — with no additional payment required and with prizes funded independently from operational funds — to engage the Gambling Act's consideration test?
2. Does the subsidiarity of the draw to a genuine game product (which operates independently of draws, markets primarily on game mechanics, and produces health behaviour change data) satisfy DIA's promotional competition guidance?
3. Does Sport Waikato's charitable trust status and primary health mandate affect the analysis?

## Things That Must Never Appear in Public-Facing Copy

**Category A — Absolute prohibitions (create consideration → chance link):**
- Any phrase suggesting the $13 fee purchases prize eligibility
- "Your subscription includes a chance to win"
- Any prize dollar amounts in headlines, hero sections, or primary CTAs
- "Lucky draw" or "lottery" (implies unregulated gambling)

**Category B — Prohibited because they misrepresent the draws (in the new model, draws are real and honest):**
- "There's no random draw" — incorrect under the new model
- "Prizes are performance-based" — incorrect under the new model
- "No element of chance" — incorrect under the new model
- Any performance-criterion language (highest minutes, longest streak) that was drafted for the old model and is now factually wrong

Do not use the old performance-award language. REAP runs honest random draws. The compliance position is that they are lawful because they are subsidiary and promotional, not because chance has been engineered out.
